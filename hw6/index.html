1. Independence in probability and in statistics required

1.1 In probability: a property of the model

Two events are independent when knowing that one occurred does not change the probability of the other:

P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B) ⇔ P(A | B) = P(A) (when P(B) > 0)

Two discrete random variables X and F are independent when every pair of their events is, i.e. the joint distribution factorises into the product of the marginals:

pᵢⱼ = P(X = xᵢ, F = fⱼ) = P(X = xᵢ)·P(F = fⱼ) = pᵢ.·p.ⱼ for all i, j
equivalently P(F = fⱼ | X = xᵢ) = P(F = fⱼ) for all i, j: every conditional distribution equals the marginal one

This is an exact statement about probabilities, which are properties of the model, not of data. It is symmetric (X independent of F ⇔ F independent of X) and stronger than zero correlation: independent variables are uncorrelated, but uncorrelated variables can be dependent.

1.2 In statistics: a decision from a sample

From data we only see frequencies. Even if X and F are independent in the population, a finite sample never satisfies nᵢⱼ / n = (nᵢ./n)(n.ⱼ/n) exactly: random sampling always produces some deviation. Statistical independence is therefore assessed with a hypothesis test:

H₀: pᵢⱼ = pᵢ.·p.ⱼ for all i, j (independence) H₁: pᵢⱼ ≠ pᵢ.·p.ⱼ for at least one cell (association)

The question becomes: are the deviations between the observed table and the table expected under independence larger than chance alone would produce? Two consequences of this change of viewpoint are essential:

  • Not rejecting H₀ does not prove independence. It only says that the data give no evidence against it; a small sample can hide a real association.
  • Rejecting H₀ does not say the association is strong (with a large n, tiny deviations become significant) nor that it is causal (§6). A measure of strength, such as Cramér's V, must accompany the p-value.

2. From records to the χ² test required

The dataset is a list of n records, each with a category of X and a category of F. The program counts, for each pair (xᵢ, fⱼ), how many records fall in it:

contingency table and margins
nᵢⱼ = #{records with X = xᵢ and F = fⱼ}, nᵢ. = Σⱼ nᵢⱼ, n.ⱼ = Σᵢ nᵢⱼ, n = Σᵢⱼ nᵢⱼ
relative frequencies
joint nᵢⱼ/n, marginal nᵢ./n and n.ⱼ/n, conditional f(fⱼ | xᵢ) = nᵢⱼ/nᵢ. and f(xᵢ | fⱼ) = nᵢⱼ/n.ⱼ
expected counts under independence — same margins, joint = product of marginals
Eᵢⱼ = n · (nᵢ./n) · (n.ⱼ/n) = nᵢ. · n.ⱼ / n
Pearson's statistic and its distribution under H₀ (for large n)
χ² = Σᵢ Σⱼ (nᵢⱼ − Eᵢⱼ)² / Eᵢⱼ ~ χ²(r−1)(c−1), p-value = P(χ²df ≥ χ²observed)
strength of association (extension)
Cramér's V = √(χ² / (n · min(r − 1, c − 1))) ∈ [0, 1]

Why (r − 1)(c − 1) degrees of freedom? Once the margins are fixed, filling (r − 1)(c − 1) cells determines the rest of the table; and r − 1 + c − 1 parameters (the marginal probabilities) are estimated from the data. Validity: the observations must be independent of each other (one record per unit), and the χ² approximation needs large expected counts — Cochran's rule: at most 20% of the Eᵢⱼ below 5 and none below 1. The p-value is computed exactly from the χ² distribution (regularised incomplete gamma function), in logarithmic form so that extremely small values are still reported.

4. What the p-value means: a permutation test optional extension

Under H₀ the pairing between X and F is arbitrary. Shuffling the F labels among the records — keeping every row and column total fixed — produces tables that independence could have generated. Repeating this B times gives the null distribution of χ² by simulation; the fraction of shuffled tables with a χ² at least as large as the observed one is a p-value that needs no large-sample approximation. It also shows that the χ²df curve describes the statistic well when expected counts are large.

5. Reading the datasets required

5.1 NSL-KDD: network connections and attacks

NSL-KDD is a classic benchmark for intrusion detection: each record is a network connection described by 41 features and labelled as normal traffic or as one of 22 attack types, grouped in four categories (DoS, Probe, R2L, U2R). I use the 25,192 connections of KDDTrain+_20Percent.

  • Protocol × label: a very strong association (χ² ≈ 2,036 with 2 df, p ≈ 10−442). ICMP connections are mostly attacks (smurf floods and ipsweep scans use ICMP), UDP connections mostly normal. Look at the residuals: the largest contributions come from ICMP and UDP, even though TCP has most records.
  • Flag × category: the connection-state flag S0 (“connection attempt seen, no reply”) is concentrated in DoS traffic: it is the signature of the SYN-flood attack neptune, which leaves half-open connections. Without grouping, many cells have tiny expected counts (rare flags, rare U2R attacks), and the page flags the approximation as unreliable: grouping or the permutation test is needed.

5.2 Berkeley 1973: the same data, opposite conclusions

Graduate admissions at UC Berkeley in 1973 (6 largest departments, 4,526 applications) are the textbook example of Simpson's paradox, studied by Bickel, Hammel and O'Connell (1975):

Aggregated, the association between gender and admission is highly significant: men were admitted at 45%, women at 30%. Within each department there is no significant difference, except department A, where women were admitted more often. The explanation is a third variable: women applied mostly to the departments with low admission rates (C–F), men mostly to those with high rates (A, B). The aggregated table mixes the effect of the department with that of gender.

5.3 Synthetic data: what independence looks like

Here X and F are generated independently by construction, so H₀ is true. With any n the test usually does not reject, and with α = 0.05 it rejects in about 5% of the seeds — the type I error rate the test promises. Change the seed to see it.

6. Statistical association is not causality required

The χ² test answers one question: are X and F statistically associated in the population that produced the data? It says nothing about why. A significant association can have several explanations other than “X causes F”:

ExplanationMechanismExample
Confoundinga third variable Z influences both X and FBerkeley: the department influences both who applies and who is admitted
Reverse causationF causes X, not the other way roundhosts with more security tools installed show more alerts — because they were compromised before and then hardened
Selection biasthe way records were collected creates the associationNSL-KDD: records were resampled by difficulty, so its class proportions do not reflect real traffic
Chancewith many tests, some are significant by luck (5% at α = 0.05)testing every pair of log fields: false associations are guaranteed
CausationX actually influences FS0 flags in DoS traffic: the SYN flood produces half-open connections — known from the TCP protocol, not from the χ² test

Establishing causation needs more than a contingency table: a randomised experiment (randomisation breaks every link with confounders), or explicit causal assumptions about the variables (e.g. conditioning on the right confounders, as the per-department analysis does), together with knowledge of the mechanism. Conversely, a missing association in the aggregate can hide real effects that cancel out across subgroups.

Why it matters in security. Detection models learn associations. A classifier trained on NSL-KDD learns that “ICMP means attack” — true in this dataset, false in a real network where ICMP is mostly harmless and attacks use other protocols. Security metrics have the same trap: “departments that completed the awareness training reported more phishing” may mean the training worked (more reporting), or that the most targeted departments were trained first. Decisions based on association alone can be wrong in either direction.

7. Conclusions

  1. The contingency table, marginal and conditional frequencies, expected counts and χ² are all computed from the raw records; the results match a reference implementation (SciPy) to all printed digits.
  2. In probability, independence is an exact factorisation pᵢⱼ = pᵢ.p.ⱼ; in statistics it is a hypothesis tested on frequencies, which can be rejected but never proven.
  3. Both NSL-KDD tables show very strong, highly significant associations between connection features and attacks; the synthetic data, independent by construction, do not.
  4. Berkeley shows that a significant association in aggregated data can disappear — or reverse — when a confounder is taken into account: the χ² test detects association, and only design and domain knowledge can turn it into a causal claim.
