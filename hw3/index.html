1. The model required

Each of the m attackers works through the n systems one after the other. At step i the attacker's penetration score changes by Xᵢ; the increments X₁, X₂, … are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.), and different attackers are independent of each other. One attacker therefore produces one trajectory S₁, S₂, …, Sₙ with Sₖ = X₁ + … + Xₖ — a random walk — and the m attackers give m independent copies of it: the sample from which we study the distributions.

1.1 The three quantities

Because the increments are independent, means and variances add: E[Sₙ] = nμ and Var(Sₙ) = nσ². Dividing by a constant c divides the variance by c²:

QuantityMeanVarianceBernoulli as n → ∞Rademacher as n → ∞
Total score Sₙnμnσ²drifts to +∞ and spreads like √nstays centred at 0, spreads like √n
Average score Sₙ/nμσ²/n→ p in probability (LLN)→ 0 in probability (LLN)
Normalised score Sₙ/√n√n·μσ²variance fixed but mean √n·p → ∞: needs centring→ N(0, 1) in distribution (CLT)

Why exactly n and √n? For Sₙ/nα the variance is σ²·n1−2α: it explodes for α < ½ and vanishes for α > ½. α = ½ is the only scaling that keeps a non-degenerate spread: √n is the natural size of the random fluctuations of a sum. This is why the Rademacher model, which has no drift (μ = 0), is the clean setting for the CLT: Sₙ/√n needs no centring.

1.2 Choosing m

m does not change the distribution of any attacker's score; it only decides how precisely the simulation estimates it:

  • a probability P is estimated with standard error √(P(1−P)/m) ≤ 0.5/√m (±2.2 percentage points at m = 2000);
  • a variance is estimated with relative error ≈ √(2/(m−1)) (≈ 3% at m = 2000, for nearly normal data);
  • the distance between an empirical CDF and the true one is of order 1/√m (95% Kolmogorov–Smirnov level 1.36/√m), while the CLT error shrinks like ≈ 0.4/√n. To see the CLT error at size n the simulation needs 1.36/√m ≪ 0.4/√n, i.e. m ≫ 10·n. Beyond that point the simulation cannot tell the true law from N(0,1) — so in §6 we also compute the exact distribution.

1.3 Run the simulation

Every attacker walks once through n = 10,000 systems; its score is recorded at n = 10, 30, 100, 300, 1,000, 3,000 and 10,000. For each fixed n the m recorded values are i.i.d. A seeded generator (xoshiro128**) makes each run reproducible: the same seed gives the same trajectories. Switch the model to compare the two cases throughout the page.

Reading. Sₙ moves (Bernoulli) and widens without limit; Sₙ/n collapses onto μ; Sₙ/√n keeps the same width — and, in the Rademacher case, the same bell shape centred at 0, while for Bernoulli it slides to the right like √n·p. The spikes for small n are not noise: Sₙ only takes integer values (for Rademacher, only values with the same parity as n), so at n = 10 the histogram shows the lattice of possible values. As n grows the lattice spacing (1/n for the average, 1/√n or 2/√n for the normalised score) becomes finer than the bins and the histogram becomes smooth.

4. Variance under the different normalisations required

Table: simulated vs theoretical variance

On a log–log scale a power law nβ is a straight line of slope β: +1 for Var(Sₙ) = nσ², −1 for Var(Sₙ/n) = σ²/n and 0 for Var(Sₙ/√n) = σ². The three slopes are the whole story of this homework: the total score accumulates uncertainty, the average loses it (LLN), and only the √n scaling keeps it constant (CLT).

5. Convergence in probability of Sₙ/n: the law of large numbers required

Weak LLN: for every ε > 0, P(|Sₙ/n − μ| ≥ ε) → 0 as n → ∞.
Chebyshev: P(|Sₙ/n − μ| ≥ ε) ≤ Var(Sₙ/n)/ε² = σ²/(nε²) → 0 (proof in one line)
Hoeffding (bounded steps, a ≤ X ≤ b): P(|Sₙ/n − μ| ≥ ε) ≤ 2·exp(−2nε²/(b−a)²)

Reading. The fraction of attackers whose success rate is more than ε away from μ falls to zero: that is what convergence in probability means — not that every trajectory is close to μ, but that the probability of being far becomes negligible. The simulation agrees with the exact binomial computation within Monte Carlo error. Chebyshev's bound is true but loose (it only uses the variance); Hoeffding's exponential bound captures the actual speed for large n. A simulated value of 0% means “below 1/m”, not exactly zero: that is where the exact computation is needed. For very small n the curve need not decrease (Rademacher, n = 10 → 30 with ε = 0.05): Sₙ/n only takes values on a grid of spacing 2/n, and which grid points fall within the tolerance changes with n. The decrease is guaranteed only asymptotically.

6. Convergence in distribution of Sₙ/√n: the central limit theorem required

CLT: Zₙ = (Sₙ − nμ)/(σ√n) → N(0, 1) in distribution, i.e. P(Zₙ ≤ z) → Φ(z) for every z.
Rademacher (μ = 0, σ = 1): Zₙ = Sₙ/√n, so Sₙ/√n → N(0, 1) with no centring.
Berry–Esseen: sup_z |P(Zₙ ≤ z) − Φ(z)| ≤ C·ρ/(σ³√n), ρ = E|X − μ|³, C ≤ 0.4748.
Distance from the normal law as n grows

Reading.

  • The histogram of Sₙ/√n matches the standard normal density: mean ≈ 0, variance ≈ 1, ≈ 5% of attackers beyond ±1.96. Skewness and excess kurtosis vanish like 1/√n and 1/n — for Rademacher the exact skewness is 0 and the excess kurtosis −2/n.
  • The exact Kolmogorov distance between the law of Zₙ and N(0,1) decreases like 1/√n and always stays below the Berry–Esseen bound: the bound gives the correct rate. For lattice variables like ours 1/√n cannot be beaten, because Fₙ jumps by P(Sₙ = s) ≈ √(2/(πn)) at the centre while Φ is continuous.
  • The simulated distance follows the exact one only until it reaches the Monte Carlo noise: even for a sample drawn from exactly N(0,1), the KS distance of m points is typically ≈ 0.87/√m (≈ 0.02 for m = 2000) and exceeds 1.36/√m (dotted line) only 5% of the time. From there on the curve is flat, because with m attackers the empirical CDF itself is uncertain by ~1/√m. This is the precise sense of “suitable m”: the larger n, the more attackers are needed to resolve the remaining non-normality (§1.2).
  • With the Bernoulli model, Sₙ/√n itself does not converge (its mean √n·p drifts away, see §3): the CLT applies to the centred and scaled (Sₙ − np)/√(np(1−p)), shown here. The skewness (1−2p)/√(np(1−p)) makes convergence slower when p is far from ½ — compare p = 0.2 with p = 0.5.

7.1 Interpretation for security

  • Large estates are predictable, small ones are not. With n = 1,000 systems an attacker's success rate is within ±2σ/√n of p (±2.5 points for p = 0.2): measuring p from incidents or red-team exercises is meaningful. With n = 10 the same rate ranges from 0% to 40% for 95% of the attackers.
  • Absolute exposure grows with the attack surface. A fixed per-system probability means a number of compromised systems growing linearly with n, with an uncertainty that also grows (like √n): bigger infrastructures are not safer, they are only easier to forecast in relative terms.
  • Plan for the best attacker, not the average one. With m independent attackers the most successful one is typically √(2 ln m) standard deviations above the mean. On small estates (large σ/√n) that lead is large; on large estates the LLN squeezes all attackers together.
  • Many weak attackers add up. Even if each attacker penetrates only a fraction p of the systems, the union covers 1 − (1−p)m of them: with p = 0.2, eleven independent attackers already reach 90%. Reducing p (hardening every system) matters more than hoping each attacker fails.
  • The fair contest (Rademacher). With no drift the average score is 0, yet the typical attacker after n steps is ≈ √n away from the start (E|Sₙ| ≈ √(2n/π)). Among m = 2,000 attackers the best one is ≈ 3.4·√n ahead: in a balanced contest, a large population of attackers still produces outliers.

7.2 Limits of the model

  • Independence between systems is the critical assumption. Shared credentials, a common vulnerable image or lateral movement make the steps positively correlated. With correlation ρ between any two steps, Var(Sₙ) = nσ²[1 + (n−1)ρ], so Var(Sₙ/n) → ρσ² instead of 0: the LLN fails and one success drags many others with it. A classic identity-management example is the same local administrator password on every workstation — which is why per-machine random passwords (e.g. Microsoft LAPS) and segmentation matter: they restore independence.
  • Identical distribution: real systems differ. With bounded but different pᵢ the LLN still holds for the average (it converges to the mean of the pᵢ), and the CLT still holds under Lindeberg's condition, but the variance becomes Σ pᵢ(1−pᵢ).
  • Independent attackers: attackers who share tools or intelligence are correlated, so their union coverage grows more slowly and their scores less independently than assumed here.

8. Conclusions

  1. Three normalisations, three behaviours: Var(Sₙ) ∝ n, Var(Sₙ/n) ∝ 1/n, Var(Sₙ/√n) constant — confirmed by simulation within Monte Carlo error.
  2. LLN: the average score converges in probability to μ; the probability of an ε-deviation follows the exact binomial value and decays exponentially (Hoeffding), much faster than Chebyshev's bound suggests.
  3. CLT: in the Rademacher case Sₙ/√n is approximately N(0, 1) already for n of a few hundred; the exact distance decays like 1/√n within the Berry–Esseen bound. In the Bernoulli case the same holds only after centring.
  4. Simulation needs enough attackers: m controls the precision of everything we estimate; to see a small CLT error, m must be much larger than n.
  5. Security reading: large estates make compromise rates predictable but not small; many attackers bring outliers and near-total coverage; and correlation between systems — not their number — is what breaks the averaging.
