Part 1

Modes of convergence in probability theory

What it means for random variables to converge, how the different meanings relate, and where each one is used.

1. Four meanings of “Xₙ → X” required

Why more than one? A random variable is a function X(ω) of the outcome ω of an experiment. For ordinary numbers “xₙ → x” has one meaning; for functions of ω there are several reasonable ones: close for (almost) every ω, close with high probability, close on average, or merely having nearly the same distribution. They are genuinely different, and each famous limit theorem uses a specific one.

In mean square (L²)E[(Xₙ − X)²] → 0 the average squared error vanishescontrols the size of errors, not just their probability: rare huge errors are not allowed
In distribution (in law, weakly)F_{Xₙ}(x) → F_X(x) at every continuity point x of F_X the laws converge; the variables need not be closehistograms look alike; Xₙ and X may even live on different sample spaces

2. How the modes relate required

  • L² ⇒ probability, by Chebyshev–Markov: P(|Xₙ − X| > ε) ≤ E[(Xₙ − X)²]/ε².
  • a.s. ⇒ probability: if paths eventually stay within ε, the probability of being outside at time n vanishes.
  • probability ⇒ distribution: if Xₙ is close to X, its CDF is close to that of X (at continuity points).
  • No other implication holds in general. The simulator below shows one counterexample for each missing arrow.
  • Partial converses: convergence in distribution to a constant implies convergence in probability; convergence in probability implies a.s. convergence along a subsequence; convergence in probability plus a bound on the variables (uniform integrability) implies convergence in mean.

Almost-sure convergence is checked through its equivalent form P(sup_{k≥n}|Xₖ − X| > ε) → 0, with the supremum over k ≤ 10,000 (a finite horizon). The four sequences: a sample mean (all modes), independent Bernoulli(1/n) (not a.s.), a single growing spike n·1{U ≤ 1/n} (not L²), and −Z compared with Z (only in distribution). In the first three examples the limit is a constant, and the “in distribution” curve coincides with the “in probability” one: for a constant limit the two modes are equivalent (§2).

Why the LLN and the CLT use different modes. The LLN is about a number μ: X̄ₙ itself gets close to it, which can be stated for the variables (in probability, a.s.). The CLT is about the shape of the fluctuations √n(X̄ₙ − μ), which never settle: the standardised sums at n and 2n have correlation 1/√2, so the sequence cannot converge in probability. Only their distribution stabilises, which is all inference needs — a confidence interval is a statement about the law of the estimator. The trajectories of HW3 §2 show exactly this: Sₙ/n settles, Sₙ/√n keeps moving with a fixed spread.

5. Why √n: additivity of variance and independence required

For any random variables with finite variance,

Var(X₁ + … + Xₙ) = Σᵢ Var(Xᵢ) + 2 Σ_{i<j} Cov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ)

If the variables are uncorrelated — independence is the usual sufficient condition — all covariances vanish and variances simply add:

Var(Sₙ) = nσ² ⇒ SD(Sₙ) = σ√n, Var(X̄ₙ) = σ²/n ⇒ SD(X̄ₙ) = σ/√n, SD(√n·(X̄ₙ − μ)) = σ

Standard deviations do not add: errors with random signs partially cancel. Geometrically, uncorrelated centred variables are orthogonal vectors in L², and lengths of orthogonal vectors add like Pythagoras — n unit steps in orthogonal directions end at distance √n, not n. Hence:

  • Probability: the sum grows like n but fluctuates like √n; √n is the only scaling of Sₙ − nμ with a finite, non-zero limit, the CLT scale.
  • Statistics: the standard error of a mean is σ/√n, confidence intervals shrink like 1/√n, and halving the uncertainty costs four times the data. Estimators whose error is of order 1/√n are called √n-consistent.

When √n fails. The additivity needs the covariances to vanish (or to be summable) and the variance to exist:

Spread of X̄ₙ across paths, measured as IQR/1.349 (equal to the SD for normal data, finite even for Cauchy data). Slope −½ on a log–log scale means 1/√n.

  • Correlation. If every pair has correlation ρ > 0, Var(X̄ₙ) = σ²(1 + (n − 1)ρ)/n → σ²ρ: the spread stops decreasing. The effective sample size is n/(1 + (n − 1)ρ) ≤ 1/ρ: with ρ = 0.01, a million observations are worth fewer than 100 independent ones.
  • Weak dependence (e.g. autocorrelated time series): the rate √n survives but σ² is replaced by the long-run variance σ²(1 + 2Σₖρₖ), usually larger.
  • No finite variance. For Cauchy data X̄ₙ has the same distribution as a single observation: no √n, and no LLN at all (E|X| = ∞).
  • In security analytics this is the pseudo-replication trap: 10,000 log events from 10 hosts are not 10,000 independent observations. Events of the same host or user are correlated, and treating them as independent produces standard errors and p-values that are far too optimistic (see also HW3 §7.2, shared credentials).
Part 2

Conditional probability, Bayes' theorem and statistical inference

How new information updates probabilities, and how Bayesian inference turns this into a method for learning from data.

6. Conditional probability in probability theory and in statistics required

In probability theory, conditioning on an event B restricts the universe to B and renormalises:

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B), P(B) > 0
multiplication rule: P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B)·P(B) = P(B | A)·P(A)
total probability: P(B) = Σᵢ P(B | Aᵢ)·P(Aᵢ) for a partition A₁, A₂, …
independence: P(A | B) = P(A) — conditioning on B changes nothing (HW6)

In statistics, conditional probabilities are what we estimate and what we condition on. The conditional relative frequencies nᵢⱼ/nᵢ. of HW6 estimate P(F = fⱼ | X = xᵢ); a regression model describes the conditional distribution of a response given predictors. Two schools give the same formula two readings:

FrequentistBayesian
Probability islong-run relative frequency of repeatable eventsa degree of belief, updated by evidence
The parameter θ isa fixed unknown constant: no probability distributionuncertain, so it has a distribution
Central objectP(data | θ): the likelihood, sampling distributions, p-valuesP(θ | data): the posterior
Typical answer“if H₀ were true, data this extreme would occur with probability p”“given the data, θ lies in [a, b] with probability 95%”

Confusing P(A | B) with P(B | A) is the most common error in reasoning with uncertainty (the prosecutor's fallacy). A p-value is P(data at least this extreme | H₀), not P(H₀ | data): the p ≈ 10⁻²² of the Berkeley data in HW6 is not the probability that there is no association. Bayes' theorem is the rule that converts one into the other.

7. Bayes' theorem: prior, likelihood, posterior required

Writing P(A ∩ B) in the two ways of the multiplication rule and dividing by P(B) gives

P(θ | data) = P(data | θ) · P(θ) / P(data), P(data) = Σ_θ P(data | θ)·P(θ) (or ∫)
posterior ∝ likelihood × prior odds form: posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio (Bayes factor)
ComponentWhat it isRole
Prior P(θ)what is believed about θ before seeing these data: past data, expert knowledge, base rates, or a deliberately vague choicestarting point; matters most when data are few
Likelihood P(data | θ)the probability of the observed data under each value of θ, read as a function of θ with the data fixed. It is not a distribution over θ and need not integrate to 1carries the information in the data; shared with frequentist methods (MLE)
Evidence P(data)the normalising constant, the same for every θmakes the posterior sum to 1; compares models
Posterior P(θ | data)the updated distribution of θeverything else follows from it: estimates, credible intervals, predictions, decisions

Why updating is central. Bayes' theorem is a learning rule: data reweight the prior by how well each value of θ explains them. Today's posterior is tomorrow's prior, and for independent data, updating one observation at a time gives exactly the same result as using all of them at once. In Bayesian inference every conclusion is a summary of the posterior, so the whole method reduces to computing it — analytically with conjugate priors, as below, or numerically (Markov chain Monte Carlo) for complex models.

8. Example 1 — Intrusion-detection alerts and the base rate required

An IDS flags malicious events. The prior is the base rate of attacks among events; the likelihoods are the detection rate P(alert | attack) and the false-positive rate P(alert | benign). The analyst wants the posterior P(attack | alert).

What it shows. With a 99% detection rate and 1% false positives, an alert on an event drawn from a base rate of 1 in 10,000 is an attack less than 1% of the time. This is the base-rate fallacy, which Axelsson (2000) identified as the main obstacle of intrusion detection: what limits a detector in practice is its false-positive rate relative to the rarity of attacks, not its detection rate. Each additional independent alert multiplies the odds by the likelihood ratio (99 here), which is why correlating evidence from different sensors works — as long as the sensors really are independent; two rules that look at the same packet are not.

9. Example 2 — Estimating a phishing click rate required

A phishing simulation is sent to n employees and k click. The unknown parameter p is the probability that an employee clicks. With a Beta(a, b) prior and a binomial likelihood L(p) ∝ pᵏ(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ, the posterior is again a Beta distribution (the Beta is conjugate to the binomial):

p ~ Beta(a, b), k | p ~ Binomial(n, p) ⇒ p | k ~ Beta(a + k, b + n − k)
posterior mean = (a + k)/(a + b + n) = w · a/(a + b) + (1 − w) · k/n, w = (a + b)/(a + b + n)

The prior acts like a + b pseudo-observations (a clicks, b non-clicks) added to the real ones; its weight w shrinks as n grows.

Presets:

How the prior influences the conclusion — try the presets with different priors:

  • Few data (2/10): the prior dominates. A uniform prior gives a wide posterior; the past-campaigns prior pulls the estimate towards 10%; the badly wrong prior gives a nonsensical answer. With little data, the choice of prior is the conclusion, and it must be justified and reported.
  • Many data (47/400): all reasonable priors give almost the same posterior, close to the frequentist estimate and interval: the data dominate.
  • No clicks (0/30): the frequentist estimate is 0% — “no risk” — which nobody believes. The posterior stays positive and gives a usable upper bound, close to the frequentist “rule of three” (3/n = 10%).
  • Decision-relevant output: P(p > tolerance | data) answers directly “how likely is it that our click rate is above the 10% we accept?” — a statement a frequentist confidence interval cannot make.

10. Example 3 — Learning step by step extension

Employees are observed one at a time and the posterior is updated after each one. Two analysts start from very different priors.

Width of the posterior: the √n law again

The two analysts disagree strongly at first, but their posteriors merge as data accumulate and both concentrate on the true value. The posterior standard deviation shrinks like 1/√n and matches the frequentist standard error √(p(1 − p)/n): this is the Bernstein–von Mises theorem — for large n the posterior is approximately normal around the MLE with variance I(θ)⁻¹/n — which links Part 2 back to the convergence results of Part 1.

11. Advantages, limitations and influence on conclusions required

AdvantagesLimitations
Uses existing knowledge (past incidents, base rates, expert judgement) explicitlyPriors can be subjective; different analysts can reach different conclusions on the same small dataset
Direct probability statements about parameters and hypotheses (“P(p > 10%) = 23%”)A misspecified or overconfident prior biases the result; a prior that gives probability 0 to a value can never learn it, whatever the data (Cromwell's rule)
Works with small samples and zero counts; regularises extreme estimatesThe likelihood is a model too: independence assumptions (e.g. naive Bayes, independent detectors) may be false

Decisions follow naturally by minimising expected loss under the posteriorBase rates are often unknown or changing (new attack campaigns), and the posterior inherits that uncertainty

Good practice: state the prior and why it was chosen; run a sensitivity analysis with several priors, as in §9; prefer weakly informative priors unless solid prior data exist; and check that conclusions are driven by the data once n is large. In security, Bayesian reasoning appears in spam filtering (naive Bayes), alert triage and risk scoring, fraud detection and threat-intelligence confidence levels.

12. Conclusions

  1. The four modes of convergence are different statements: a.s. and L² both imply convergence in probability, which implies convergence in distribution, and nothing else holds in general — each missing implication has a simple counterexample.
  2. The laws of large numbers are statements about the variables (in probability, a.s., L²); the CLT is a statement about distributions only, and that is enough for confidence intervals and tests.
  3. √n comes from the additivity of variance for uncorrelated variables: standard deviations of sums grow like √n. Correlation, dependence or infinite variance break it, and treating dependent observations as independent overstates precision.
  4. Bayes' theorem turns a prior and a likelihood into a posterior; the posterior is the whole of Bayesian inference. The base rate (prior) can dominate the conclusion, as with IDS alerts; with enough data the likelihood dominates, and different priors converge.
