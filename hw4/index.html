Exercise 1

Welford's recurrence formulas

Derive the recurrences for the sum of squares and the variance with the same approach as Knuth's recurrence for the mean, and code them.

1. Why a recurrence?

The textbook definitions x̄ = (1/n)Σxᵢ and s² = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²/(n − 1) need all the data: the mean must be known before the squared deviations can be summed (two passes). A recurrence updates the statistics one observation at a time, so that:

  • data can be processed as a stream (log events, network measurements) in a single pass, with O(1) memory and nothing stored;
  • the current mean and variance are available at every step, not only at the end;
  • the computation is numerically stable, unlike the one-pass shortcut Σx² − (Σx)²/n (§7).

2. Knuth's recurrence for the mean required

Let Mₖ be the mean of the first k observations. Separate the last one from the others:

Mₖ = (1/k) Σᵢ₌₁ᵏ xᵢ = ((k − 1)·Mₖ₋₁ + xₖ) / k = Mₖ₋₁ + (xₖ − Mₖ₋₁)/k, M₀ = 0

The pattern is new value = old value + correction, where the correction depends only on the new observation's deviation from the old mean. No sum is ever stored, so it never grows large.

3. Welford's recurrence for the sum of squares required

Let SSₖ = Σᵢ₌₁ᵏ (xᵢ − Mₖ)² be the sum of squared deviations from the current mean. We look for SSₖ in terms of SSₖ₋₁, exactly as above.

Step 1 — expand the square
SSₖ = Σ xᵢ² − 2Mₖ Σ xᵢ + k·Mₖ² = Σᵢ₌₁ᵏ xᵢ² − k·Mₖ² (because Σ xᵢ = k·Mₖ)
Step 2 — subtract the same expression for k − 1: the common part Σᵢ₌₁ᵏ⁻¹ xᵢ² cancels
SSₖ − SSₖ₋₁ = xₖ² − k·Mₖ² + (k − 1)·Mₖ₋₁²
Step 3 — write everything with d = xₖ − Mₖ₋₁ and Knuth's formula Mₖ = Mₖ₋₁ + d/k
xₖ² = Mₖ₋₁² + 2Mₖ₋₁d + d²
k·Mₖ² = k·Mₖ₋₁² + 2Mₖ₋₁d + d²/k
Step 4 — substitute: the Mₖ₋₁² and Mₖ₋₁d terms cancel
SSₖ − SSₖ₋₁ = Mₖ₋₁² + 2Mₖ₋₁d + d² − k·Mₖ₋₁² − 2Mₖ₋₁d − d²/k + (k − 1)·Mₖ₋₁² = d² − d²/k = d²·(k − 1)/k
Step 5 — recognise the new deviation: xₖ − Mₖ = d − d/k = d·(k − 1)/k, hence d²(k − 1)/k = d·(xₖ − Mₖ)
SSₖ = SSₖ₋₁ + (xₖ − Mₖ₋₁)·(xₖ − Mₖ), SS₀ = 0

The correction is the product of the new observation's deviation from the old mean and from the new mean. Both are small numbers of the order of σ, which is the source of the stability shown in §7. Check at k = 1: M₁ = x₁, so SS₁ = (x₁ − 0)(x₁ − x₁) = 0, as it must be.

4. Recurrences for the variance required

The variance is SS divided by a count. Dividing the recurrence above (with SSₖ₋₁ = (k − 2)s²ₖ₋₁ and d·(xₖ − Mₖ) = d²(k − 1)/k):

Sample variance (unbiased): s²ₖ = SSₖ/(k − 1), s²ₖ = ((k − 2)/(k − 1))·s²ₖ₋₁ + d²/k for k ≥ 2
Population variance (plug-in): σ̂²ₖ = SSₖ/k, σ̂²ₖ = ((k − 1)/k)·σ̂²ₖ₋₁ + d²(k − 1)/k²

In practice one updates SS (a single product) and divides only when the variance is needed: it is cheaper and avoids accumulating rounding in the (k − 2)/(k − 1) factor. Why k − 1 and not k is the subject of Ex. 2 (§8).

5. Code required

The core logic is three lines; everything else is bookkeeping. JavaScript, as used on this page (welford.js):

let k = 0, mean = 0, ss = 0;
function push(x) {
  k++;
  const d = x - mean;        // deviation from the OLD mean
  mean += d / k;             // Knuth
  ss += d * (x - mean);      // Welford: old deviation × new deviation
}
const variance = () => ss / (k - 1);   // sample variance, k ≥ 2

The same in Python:

k, mean, ss = 0, 0.0, 0.0
for x in data:
    k += 1
    d = x - mean
    mean += d / k
    ss += d * (x - mean)
variance = ss / (k - 1)

extension welford.js also contains the merge rule of Chan, Golub and LeVeque: two accumulators (nA, MA, SSA) and (nB, MB, SSB) combine with δ = MB − MA into SS = SSA + SSB + δ²·nAnB/n — useful when data are split across machines or log shards.

7. Numerical stability extension

10,000 scores uniform on [0, 10] (true variance ≈ ), shifted by a constant c. Adding a constant does not change the variance, so every method should return the same value:

Why the shortcut fails. Σx² and (Σx)²/n are two huge, nearly equal numbers whose difference is the small quantity of interest. With c = 10⁸ each x² is ≈ 10¹⁶, where consecutive double-precision numbers are 2 apart; after 10,000 additions the rounding error is far larger than SS ≈ 8·10⁴ itself, and the result can even be negative — an impossible variance. This is catastrophic cancellation. Welford only ever handles deviations d of the order of σ, so its error is governed by σ, not by the size of the data. The small residual error for very large c comes partly from the data themselves: near 10¹⁰ a double can only represent multiples of ≈ 2·10⁻⁶, so the scores are already rounded before any algorithm sees them.

Exercise 2

Sampling mean and variance

Extension of HW3: each penetration attempt now gets a score between 0 and 10. m hackers, n systems each, Welford for every hacker, then averages across hackers compared with μ and σ².

8. Model and theory required

Hacker j attempts systems 1, …, n and gets scores X₁⁽ʲ⁾, …, Xₙ⁽ʲ⁾, i.i.d. from a fixed distribution on [0, 10] with mean μ and variance σ²; hackers are independent. For each hacker, Welford's recurrences give the sampling mean x̄ⱼ and sampling variance s²ⱼ of its n scores. Each of them is a random variable: a different trajectory would give different values. Their distribution across hackers is the sampling distribution.

My choice of distribution is the right-skewed discrete one (most attempts score low, a full 10 is rare), because a skewed, non-normal distribution is the realistic case and makes the effects of small n visible. The others can be selected for comparison. Each hacker's scores are fed to Welford's recurrences one at a time, so a single pass of 1,000 systems yields x̄ and s² at n = 2, 3, 5, …, 1,000 (prefixes of the same trajectory).

What theory predicts, for i.i.d. scores:

E[x̄] = μ Var(x̄) = σ²/n
E[SS] = (n − 1)σ² because SS = Σ(xᵢ − μ)² − n(x̄ − μ)², so E[SS] = nσ² − n·σ²/n
E[s²] = E[SS/(n − 1)] = σ² E[SS/n] = σ²(n − 1)/n (biased low)
Var(s²) = μ₄/n − σ⁴(n − 3)/(n(n − 1)), μ₄ = E[(X − μ)⁴]

The n − 1 (Bessel's correction) appears because the deviations are measured from x̄, which is itself fitted to the same data and is therefore closer to the points than μ is: one “degree of freedom” is used up by estimating the mean.

9. Averages across hackers vs μ and σ² required

✓ = the average is within 3 standard errors of the theoretical value (Monte Carlo SE = theoretical SD / √m).

Average of the sampling means vs μ
Average of the sampling variances vs σ²

What the comparison shows.

  • The average of the m sampling means equals μ within Monte Carlo error for every n, even n = 2: the sampling mean is an unbiased estimator of μ.
  • The average of the sampling variances s² = SS/(n − 1) equals σ² within Monte Carlo error, again for every n: s² is unbiased for σ².
  • Dividing by n instead gives an average of σ²(n − 1)/n: half of the true variance at n = 2, 10% too low at n = 10, a negligible difference at n = 1,000. The bias is a small-sample problem.
  • The average of the sampling standard deviations s stays below σ for small n: an unbiased s² does not give an unbiased s, because the square root is concave (Jensen's inequality: E[√s²] ≤ √E[s²]).
  • Agreement holds on average across hackers, not for each hacker: a single hacker's x̄ or s² can be far from μ or σ² (§10). Unbiasedness is a property of the procedure, observed over many repetitions.

10. Effect of the number of systems n on the variability required

Running sampling mean of 30 hackers (band: μ ± 2σ/√k)
Running sampling variance of the same hackers (band: σ² ± 2√Var(s²), from k = 2)

Reading.

  • Increasing n does not change where the estimates are centred (they are unbiased at every n); it narrows their spread. On the log–log chart both variances fall with slope −1: Var(x̄) = σ²/n exactly, Var(s²) ≈ (μ₄ − σ⁴)/n. Their standard deviations shrink like 1/√n, so to halve the uncertainty a hacker must attempt four times as many systems. This is consistency: the estimates converge to μ and σ² (LLN, HW3).
  • In relative terms the sampling variance is usually the noisier estimate: its relative standard deviation is ≈ √((κ − 1)/n), where κ = μ₄/σ⁴ is the kurtosis, so heavy-tailed score distributions make the variance hard to estimate even when the mean is easy.
  • For small n the distribution of s² is strongly right-skewed: most hackers get s² below σ² and a few get very large values, which keep the average right. The distribution of x̄ becomes normal quickly (CLT); that of s² needs much larger n.
  • m plays a different role: it does not change any hacker's variability; it only makes the averages across hackers more precise (SE ∝ 1/√m).

11. Why these results matter for inferential statistics required

In practice we observe one sample, not m of them. The experiment shows what happens over all the samples we could have observed, which is exactly what inference relies on:

  • Unbiased estimators. x̄ and s² are right on average, so they are the natural point estimates of μ and σ², whatever the (unknown) distribution.
  • Standard errors. Var(x̄) = σ²/n tells us how far a single x̄ is likely to be from μ; replacing σ² by its unbiased estimate s² gives the standard error s/√n, computable from the sample alone.
  • Confidence intervals and tests. Combining the CLT (x̄ approximately normal) with s gives x̄ ± t·s/√n and the t statistic (x̄ − μ₀)/(s/√n). Both depend on the two facts verified here.
  • Pooling. The average of the hackers' s² is the pooled variance estimate used when several groups share the same σ² (e.g. in ANOVA).
  • Sample-size planning. Because uncertainty falls like 1/√n, one can choose n in advance to reach a target precision.

12. Security reading extension

  • Ranking attackers or red teams. A hacker's mean score over a few systems is a poor measure of skill: with n = 5, x̄ has standard deviation σ/√5 — here more than one score point — so rankings based on few attempts mostly reflect luck.
  • Behavioural baselines. User and entity behaviour analytics (UEBA) and SIEM rules keep, for each user or host, a running mean and variance of some activity (logons per hour, data volume) and raise an alert when z = (x − x̄)/s is large. Welford is how such baselines are maintained on streams, without storing raw events — also a data-minimisation benefit. Section 10 shows the risk: with a short history s² is often underestimated, so z is inflated and new accounts generate false positives. A sensible rule is to raise thresholds or suppress alerts until enough observations exist.
  • Heavy tails. When rare extreme values exist (μ₄ large), variance estimates converge slowly and a single outlier can move them: robust alternatives (median, MAD) are often preferred for detection baselines.
  • Limits. As in HW3, the results assume i.i.d. scores; real attackers learn from previous systems, which makes scores dependent and the effective sample size smaller than n.

13. Conclusions

  1. Welford's recurrence SSₖ = SSₖ₋₁ + (xₖ − Mₖ₋₁)(xₖ − Mₖ) follows from Knuth's recurrence for the mean with a few lines of algebra and needs only three lines of code; it is single-pass, constant-memory and numerically stable, unlike Σx² − (Σx)²/n.
  2. Across m hackers, the average sampling mean matches μ and the average sampling variance (denominator n − 1) matches σ² for every n: both estimators are unbiased. Dividing by n underestimates σ² by the factor (n − 1)/n.
  3. Increasing n leaves the centre unchanged and shrinks the spread like 1/√n; the variance estimate is noisier than the mean and skewed for small n.
  4. These are the foundations of standard errors, confidence intervals and tests — and, in security monitoring, of baselines that are only as reliable as the history behind them.
